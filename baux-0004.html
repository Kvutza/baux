TCV results and experiment setup

TCV Rampdown: Comparing Search Configurations

The toka_gym rampdown environment supplies the objective for this study. The plasma-rampdown problem and its simulation setting are described by Wang et al. [Wan+25] and Theiler et al. [The+26]. Here, baux searches the weights of an \(11 \to 256 \to 256 \to 256 \to 8\) feedback policy. Its \(136{,}712\) parameters enter a rollout, and the resulting error becomes the objective value.

We compare eight configurations over 10,000 rounds with ten paired seeds: four sharing policies for ten-region search without a surrogate, and four distance choices for one-region ENN search. The recorded labels are TuRBO-zero and TuRBO-ENN; the former uses the TuRBO region controller without the original algorithm's GP surrogate. Each round evaluates one proposed policy. The budget therefore covers 800,000 proposed-policy evaluations across the configurations and seeds.

All four surrogate-free sharing configurations have the same recorded trajectory and finish at mean error 1.3112 ± 0.0157. The ENN configurations finish between 1.3409 ± 0.0301 (wasserstein) and 1.3693 ± 0.0303 (conformal). Here and in the figure, ± denotes the standard error of the mean across ten seeds; lower error is better.

Holm-adjusted two-sided sign-flip tests against origin_only give \(p \ge 0.738\) at round 10,000; all four surrogate-free sharing configurations follow identical trajectories in this run.

TORAX: A Transport Objective

TORAX evolves radial plasma transport profiles (Citrin et al. [Cit+24]). baux includes an evaluator that varies heating power and deposition location, runs the solver, and returns a negative loss based on central electron temperature, ion temperature and heating power. This gives the optimizer a concrete simulation objective with two bounded inputs.

Device Geometry and Equilibria

The console displays vessel and magnet geometry for TCV, SPARC, ARC, ITER and DIII-D. The non-TCV views include exported equilibrium and engineering data from FUSE (Meneghini et al. [Men+24]).

Synthetic Objectives

Choose from 56 test functions in the explorer below to inspect their domains and surfaces. To use the same catalogue in your own search, load a function through baux.benchmarks.oxcaml.

From Python, baux.benchmarks.oxcaml returns bounds and an objective callable. The following example evaluates Ackley in 16 dimensions, then checks the evaluator at the origin:

import numpy as np
import baux
from baux.benchmarks import oxcaml

bounds, objective = oxcaml.problem("ackley", dim=16, workers=4)
state = baux.init(bounds, baux.enn(k=10) | baux.ucb(beta=2.0), key=baux.key(0))
for _ in range(12):
    state, batch = baux.ask(state, num_arms=8)
    state, _ = baux.tell(state, batch, objective(batch.points))

grid = np.zeros((4096, 16), dtype=np.float64)
vals = oxcaml.eval_batch("ackley", grid, backend="simd", workers=4)
assert vals.shape == (4096,) and np.allclose(vals, 0.0, atol=1e-12)
print(f"4096 evals verified; TuRBO-ENN best_y={state.best_y:.5f}")

Pass backend="scalar" or backend="simd" with workers to configure native parallel evaluation; see the benchmarking note for prepared-array versus end-to-end timings.
